Course · Retrieval-augmented generation

Every chunk deserves to remember where it came from

Contextual Retrieval is a deceptively simple technique published by Anthropic: before you index a chunk, have an LLM write one sentence saying where the chunk sits in its document — then index the sentence with the chunk. This course walks through why that works, what it costs, and the measured difference it makes, using a real implementation you can run.

01Why chunks fail

Every RAG pipeline begins with an act of violence: it takes a coherent document and cuts it into chunks small enough to embed and retrieve. The cut is necessary — you can't stuff whole filings into every prompt — but something real is lost. Look at a typical chunk from a quarterly report:

“Revenue grew 14% year over year, reaching $595 million for the quarter, driven by continued strength in subscription renewals.” Chunk 7 of 10 · some document

Whose revenue? Which quarter? The document knew — the title said Borealis Industries, Q2 2025 — but the chunk doesn't. The company name lives three pages away, in a heading that landed in a different chunk.

This isn't a corner case. It is the normal condition of financial filings, contracts, medical records, and papers: documents state their identity once, at the top, and then spend fifty pages saying “the Company”, “the quarter”, “the patient”. Almost every chunk in the body is an orphan.

Now imagine a user asks: “How much did Borealis Industries' revenue grow in Q2 2025?” The chunk above is the answer. But the systems doing the searching only see the chunk as written — and as written, it matches “Borealis” and “Q2 2025” not at all. If your corpus holds twelve near-identical quarterly reports, there are twelve interchangeable revenue chunks, and the retriever is reduced to guessing. Measured on exactly this corpus shape (§10), a standard pipeline finds the right chunk in its top-5 less than 3% of the time. It doesn't degrade. It collapses.

02Retrieval, in one page

To see why the fix works, you need thirty seconds on how the two standard retrievers actually think.

Dense retrieval — search by meaning

An embedding model turns text into a vector — a point in a high-dimensional space arranged so that texts with similar meaning land near each other. Index every chunk's vector; at query time, embed the question and take the nearest chunks by cosine similarity. Dense retrieval is superb at paraphrase (“How fast did sales climb?” finds “revenue grew”) and even works across languages. Its weakness: it's blurry on exact things — identifiers, error codes, names. And critically, it can only embed what's in the text. If the chunk never says “Borealis”, no geometry will put it near Borealis questions.

BM25 — search by exact words

BM25 is the classic keyword scorer (the heart of every serious search engine since the '90s). It rewards chunks that contain the query's rare terms — the rarer the term corpus-wide, the bigger the reward, with diminishing returns for repetition and a penalty for long chunks. It's exact where embeddings are blurry: perfect for “error TS-1129” or an accented product name. Its weakness is the mirror image: it's blind to synonyms — and, again, it can only match words that are actually in the chunk.

The shared blind spot

Dense and BM25 fail differently on almost everything — which is why hybrids of the two work so well (§7). But they share exactly one blind spot: neither can retrieve on information the chunk doesn't contain. An orphaned chunk is invisible to both. That shared blind spot is the entire target of Contextual Retrieval.

03The idea: give every chunk a memory

The fix is almost embarrassingly direct. At ingestion time — once per chunk, before anything is indexed — show an LLM the whole document and the chunk, and ask it to write a one-to-two-sentence blurb situating the chunk within the document: 

<document>
{THE WHOLE DOCUMENT}
</document>

Here is the chunk we want to situate within the whole document:
<chunk>{THE CHUNK}</chunk>

Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within
the overall document for the purposes of improving search retrieval
of the chunk. Answer only with the succinct context and nothing else.

That is Anthropic's published prompt, verbatim. For our orphaned revenue chunk, the model — which has read the title page — writes something like:

Generated blurb — prepended for indexing This chunk is from Borealis Industries' Q2 2025 quarterly report, in the Revenue section covering year-over-year growth.
Original chunk — unchanged Revenue grew 14% year over year, reaching $595 million for the quarter, driven by continued strength in subscription renewals.

The blurb restores exactly what the cut destroyed: the company, the period, the section. Prepend it to the chunk and suddenly both retrievers can see it — the embedding of blurb + chunk lands near Borealis-Q2 questions, and BM25 finds “Borealis” as a literal token. One LLM call repaired the shared blind spot from §2.

Notice what the technique is not. It's not a smarter chunker — the chunks are unchanged. It's not a bigger context window — the answer model sees the same chunks it always did. It's a metadata enrichment at indexing time, done by the one component in the stack that has actually read the whole document.

04What gets indexed vs. what gets cited

The single most important design rule — the one that keeps the technique honest — is that the blurb exists for finding, not for reading. It's indexed, never cited. One decision, stated once, and every stage inherits it:

StageSees blurb + chunkSees original chunk only
Embedding✓
BM25 index✓
Reranker✓
Answer generation✓
Citations shown to the user✓

Why so strict? Because the blurb is generated text. It's usually right, but it is a model's paraphrase, not the source. Letting it leak into answers or citations would mean quoting the model to the user while claiming to quote the document. Keeping it index-only gives you the findability without ever polluting provenance: every citation still resolves to real bytes of the real document.

The one non-obvious row is the reranker — it sees the blurb too, and this was learned the hard way (§8).

05Paying for it: prompt caching

The obvious objection: one LLM call per chunk, each carrying the entire document? A 100-page document with 200 chunks means sending those 100 pages two hundred times. Naïvely, that's ruinous.

The rescue is prompt-prefix caching, and it's why the prompt in §3 puts the document first. Modern LLM providers cache the computation for a request's opening tokens: if two requests begin with byte-identical text, the second one gets that prefix served from cache at roughly a tenth of the price. Since every chunk of a document shares the same <document>…</document> opening, the document is paid for in full exactly once — every subsequent chunk pays cached rates for it and full price only for its own small tail.

Call 1 — writes the cache
document (full price) + chunk 1 + instructions
Calls 2…N — read the cache
document (~10% price, cached) + chunk k + instructions

Document-first ordering isn't cosmetic; it is the economics. Measured on the implementation this course accompanies: 82% of prompt tokens served from cache in steady state. Two operational details worth knowing:

Prime before you parallelize. The cache entry doesn't exist until the first call writes it. Fan out all chunks at once and every worker pays full price for the prefix simultaneously. The right shape: run the first chunk of each document alone, then fan out.

Verify, don't assume. Providers return a cached_tokens count per call. Surface it in your ingestion reports — a caching regression should be a number you notice, not a bill you discover.

06When the document doesn't fit

The published recipe assumes the whole document fits in the model's input window. A 500k-token filing doesn't. And well before hard limits, blurb quality quietly degrades as irrelevant bulk grows — the “context rot” problem. So oversized documents need a fallback, and it should degrade gracefully, not truncate blindly.

The implementation's answer is excerpt mode. Instead of the whole document, each chunk's prompt carries a purpose-built excerpt: 

excerpt = document HEAD   # ~6k tokens: title, TOC, intro — the document's identity
        + "[…]"
        + REGION around the chunk   # ± ~4k-token margins of local context

The head keeps what §1 showed matters most — the document's identity (who, what period, what kind of document) — while the region supplies local context. And to keep §5's economics alive, consecutive chunks are batched so that each batch shares one byte-identical excerpt: prefix caching keeps working per batch exactly as it worked per document. The prompt honestly says <document_excerpt> rather than pretending the model saw everything.

07Hybrid search & rank fusion

With contextual text indexed on both sides, run both retrievers on every query — dense for meaning, BM25 for exact terms — and merge. But merging raises a trap: a cosine similarity of 0.83 and a BM25 score of 7.1 live on incomparable scales. Adding or averaging them is numerology.

The clean merge ignores scores entirely and uses ranks — Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Each chunk earns Σ 1 / (60 + rank) across every list it appears in: 

# chunk ranked 1st by dense, 3rd by BM25:
score = 1/(60+1) + 1/(60+3) = 0.0323   # liked by both → rises
# chunk ranked 1st by dense only:
score = 1/(60+1)            = 0.0164   # loved by one → beaten

A chunk both retrievers liked outranks a chunk either loved alone — which is the behavior you actually want, since the two fail so differently. The constant 60 just softens the top ranks' dominance; nobody tunes it, everybody uses it.

One deflationary note from measurement: hybrid is a refinement, not the rescue. On the benchmark corpus, plain-chunk hybrid still scores ~0% — BM25 matches the company name only in title chunks, which contain the name but not the metric, and fusion faithfully inherits the confusion. Garbage ranked two ways is still garbage. The blurb is what makes hybrid worth having.

08Reranking: similarity is not relevance

Retrieval scores how close a chunk is to the query; it can't tell whether the chunk actually answers it. After fusion, the top candidates for a Borealis-Q2-revenue question typically include Borealis Q1's revenue section and Borealis Q2's margin section — all near, only one right. A reranker fixes this: it reads the query and the candidates together and reorders by answer-likelihood. One cheap listwise LLM call over the top ~30 fused candidates is enough; hosted cross-encoders are the higher-throughput alternative.

Measured lesson — rerank the contextual text

The reranker must be shown blurb + chunk, not the bare chunk. In an early version of the implementation it saw bare chunks — and it made results worse (Pass@5 fell 94% → 81%): out-of-context chunks are as indistinguishable to a reranker as they were to the retrievers, so it confidently reshuffled twelve identical-looking revenue chunks. Shown the contextual text, the same reranker became the best stage in the pipeline. The lesson generalizes: every stage that judges a chunk needs the chunk's context to judge it.

Engineering note: LLM rerankers are flaky — they garble indices and forget candidates. Treat their output defensively: drop invalid indices, append forgotten candidates in their original order. A flaky reranker may reorder your results; it must never be able to lose one.

09Grounded answers

The last stage hands the top chunks — original text only, per §4 — to the answer model as numbered sources, under a contract: use only these sources; cite inline as [S1], [S2]; if the sources don't contain the answer, say so plainly. Every [S#] resolves to a document, page, and section, so every claim is checkable.

The contract matters most when retrieval fails. Watch both pipelines answer “How much did Borealis Industries' revenue grow in Q2 2025?”:

Plain pipeline — retrieval failed

“The provided sources do not include Borealis Industries' revenue figures for Q2 2025, so I can't determine how much revenue grew from them alone. [S1] [S3]”

Contextual pipeline — retrieval succeeded

“Borealis Industries' total revenue grew 14% year over year in Q2 2025, reaching $595 million for the quarter [S2].”

The plain pipeline retrieved the wrong chunks — and refused rather than hallucinated. That's the grounding contract holding under failure. The contextual pipeline retrieved the right chunk and answered with the exact figure and a citation that resolves to the right section. Retrieval quality and answer honesty are separate systems; you want both.

10The proof

Does it work? Here is an A/B measurement designed to isolate exactly one variable. Corpus: 12 synthetic quarterly reports (3 companies × 4 quarters), structurally identical, differing only in their numbers — and, like real filings, never repeating the company name inside section bodies. 36 queries of the form “How much did {company}'s {metric} change in {quarter}?”, gold chunks computed by construction. Both conditions index the identical chunks with the identical models; the only difference is whether the blurb was prepended. Pass@k: did any gold chunk make the top k?

ConfigurationPass@1Pass@3Pass@5
Plain chunks — dense only0.0%2.8%2.8%
Plain chunks — hybrid0.0%0.0%2.8%
Contextual — dense only55.6%80.6%91.7%
Contextual — hybrid66.7%86.1%91.7%
Contextual — hybrid + rerank91.7%97.2%97.2%

Read it bottom-up. Retrieval failures at k=5 drop from 97.2% to 2.8% — a 97% reduction. The blurb does the heavy lifting (2.8% → 91.7% Pass@5 on dense alone); hybrid earns its keep only after the blurb gives BM25 real terms to match (Pass@1 55.6% → 66.7%); the reranker delivers the decisive precision lift (Pass@1 66.7% → 91.7%).

Calibrate against Anthropic's own evaluation on a natural corpus (737 code chunks, 248 queries): Pass@10 rose 87% → 92% with contextual embeddings, ~93% adding BM25, ~95% adding a reranker — a 49% failure reduction (67% with reranking). The synthetic numbers above are starker because the corpus is built to maximize chunk ambiguity; real corpora sit in between, closer to Anthropic's figures the more self-contained their chunks already are.

11Limits, and when to reach for it

A course that ends in a sales pitch has taught you nothing. The honest boundary conditions:

It targets one failure mode. Contextual Retrieval repairs chunks that lost their document context. If your chunks are already self-contained — FAQ entries, product descriptions, short standalone notes — the blurbs add cost and near-zero lift. Diagnose first: pull ten random chunks and ask, “could a stranger tell what this is about?” If yes, you don't need this.

It costs real money once. One LLM call per chunk at ingestion. With caching (§5) it's roughly a tenth of the naïve cost, and it's a one-time capital expense per document — but for a corpus you re-ingest hourly, do that math.

It doesn't fix bad chunking, parsing, or answering. A blurb can't rescue a chunk that tore a table in half, a PDF whose figures were silently dropped, or an answer model with no grounding contract. It's one stage of a pipeline; the measured 97.2% needed all of them.

When the shoe fits, though, it fits remarkably well: long documents that state their identity once and assume it thereafter — filings, contracts, reports, manuals, papers — with questions that name entities the chunks omit. That's a large fraction of the documents enterprises actually care about, and on them this one-sentence-per-chunk technique is among the highest-leverage changes you can make to a RAG system.
