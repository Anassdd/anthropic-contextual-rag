Every chunk deserves to remember where it came from

You can code, but you've never built a retrieval system — this course is for you. We'll build the ideas one at a time, with real inputs and real outputs at every step: what a chunk actually looks like, why search misses it, the one-sentence fix Anthropic published, what it honestly costs, and the caching trick that makes it affordable. By the end you should be able to decide, on evidence, whether you even want this.

01What RAG actually is

A language model doesn't know your documents. It knows its training data, which does not include last quarter's report, your contracts, or your internal wiki. RAG — retrieval-augmented generation — is the standard fix, and the whole idea fits in three steps:

1. When a user asks a question, find the few passages in your documents most likely to contain the answer.
2. Paste those passages into the prompt.
3. Ask the model to answer using only those passages, citing them.

The model never "learns" your documents. It reads a handful of relevant excerpts at question time — an open-book exam, where retrieval decides which pages are open.

Why not just paste all the documents into the prompt?

Three reasons. Documents outgrow context windows fast (one long filing can be 500k tokens). You'd pay to resend your whole corpus with every question. And models get measurably worse at using a fact buried in a mountain of mostly-irrelevant text. If your entire corpus is under ~100k tokens, though — genuinely, just put it in the prompt and skip RAG. Machinery you don't need is machinery that breaks.

Everything therefore hangs on step 1. If retrieval brings back the wrong passages, the best model in the world politely answers from the wrong material. Retrieval quality is the ceiling on answer quality — and this course is about one high-leverage way to raise that ceiling.

02Chunking: the necessary crime

You can't retrieve "the relevant part of a document" without deciding what a part is. So documents get cut into chunks — passages of a few hundred tokens, small enough to index and paste into a prompt. Don't take our word for what that looks like; toggle it:

Each chunk keeps provenance — which document, which pages, which heading trail — because later, when the model cites a chunk, that citation has to resolve to something a human can check.

A good chunker cuts along real structure: headings first, then paragraphs, then sentences. And some things it must refuse to cut at all — a code block, a formula, a table. Half a table row is worse than an oversized chunk, always.

Isn't there some smarter, AI-powered way to chunk?

"Semantic chunking" (using embeddings to find topic boundaries) exists, and this library deliberately doesn't use it: it's slower and not consistently better. Chunking is a low-leverage cut. The retrieval gains in this course come from what happens after the cut — which is the whole point of the next three sections.

03How a chunk gets found

Two search mechanisms with opposite personalities. Serious systems run both, and you need a feel for each to see where they both fail.

Embeddings — search by meaning

An embedding model turns text into a long list of numbers (a vector), arranged so that texts with similar meaning get nearby numbers. "Revenue grew 14%" and "sales climbed by double digits" land close together; "the printer is jammed" lands far away. You embed every chunk once at ingestion; at question time you embed the question and grab the nearest chunks. This is dense retrieval, and its superpower is paraphrase: the user never has to guess the document's exact wording. Its weakness: it's blurry on exact strings — product codes, error IDs, names.

BM25 — search by exact words

BM25 is the classic keyword scorer that has powered search engines for decades. It rewards chunks containing the question's rare words: if only three chunks in the corpus mention "TS-1129", a query with "TS-1129" nails them instantly. Exact where embeddings are blurry; blind to synonyms where embeddings shine.

That last row is the entire setup for this course. Both retrievers, for all their differences, can only work with the text that's actually in the chunk. Hold that thought and look at chunk 2 again.

04Where it all breaks down

Here is chunk 2, exactly as the index sees it:

"Total revenue grew 14% year over year, reaching $595 million for the quarter, driven by continued strength in subscription renewals." chunk 2 of 4 · some document

Now the user asks: "How much did Borealis Industries' revenue grow in Q2 2025?"

That chunk is the answer. But read it as a stranger: whose revenue? Which quarter? The words "Borealis" and "Q2 2025" appear nowhere in it — the document said them once, on the title line, which landed in chunk 0. So the embedding of chunk 2 has no Borealis in it to be near the question, and BM25 has no "Borealis" token to match. Both retrievers are blind to the right answer, for the same reason.

Now imagine the realistic version: your corpus holds twelve companies' quarterly reports. It contains twelve interchangeable "revenue grew X%" chunks, and retrieval degenerates into a coin toss among them. This isn't a corner case — it's how real documents are written. They state their identity once, then say "the Company", "the quarter", "the patient" for fifty pages. Nearly every chunk in the body is an orphan.

Measured on exactly this corpus shape (§12), standard hybrid retrieval finds a correct chunk in its top-5 2.8% of the time. It doesn't degrade. It collapses.

Try this on your own corpus

Pull ten random chunks and read them cold. For each one, ask: "could a stranger tell what document this came from and what it's about?" Mostly no → you have this problem, and the rest of this course will move your numbers a lot. Mostly yes → the fix below will help only at the margin (we'll be honest about that in §13).

05The fix: a sticky note on every chunk

Anthropic's published fix is almost embarrassingly direct. At ingestion — once per chunk, before anything is indexed — show an LLM the whole document and the chunk, and ask for one or two sentences situating the chunk within it. This is the prompt, verbatim: 

<document>
{THE WHOLE DOCUMENT}
</document>

Here is the chunk we want to situate within the whole document:
<chunk>{THE CHUNK}</chunk>

Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within
the overall document for the purposes of improving search retrieval
of the chunk. Answer only with the succinct context and nothing else.

The model — which has read the title line — writes something like: "This chunk is from Borealis Industries' Q2 2025 quarterly report, in the Revenue section covering year-over-year growth." That blurb is prepended to the chunk for indexing only. See the difference from the index's point of view:

Interactive · what the index sees
Total revenue grew 14% year over year, reaching $595 million for the quarter, driven by continued strength in subscription renewals. Net revenue retention remained above target.

No company. No quarter. To a query naming "Borealis" and "Q2 2025", this chunk is invisible — to embeddings and keywords.

This chunk is from Borealis Industries' Q2 2025 quarterly report, in the Revenue section covering year-over-year growth. Total revenue grew 14% year over year, reaching $595 million for the quarter, driven by continued strength in subscription renewals. Net revenue retention remained above target.

The highlighted blurb is what got added. Now the embedding lands near Borealis-Q2 questions, and BM25 has "Borealis" as a literal token to match. Everything below the blurb is untouched.

Notice what this technique is not. Not a smarter chunker — the chunks are unchanged. Not a bigger context window — the answer model sees the same chunks it always did. It's a metadata enrichment at indexing time, written by the one component in the stack that has actually read the whole document. One LLM call repaired the shared blind spot from §3.

06Indexed vs. cited — the honesty rule

The single most important design rule, the one that keeps the technique trustworthy: the blurb exists for finding, not for reading. One decision, stated once, and every stage inherits it:

StageSees blurb + chunkSees original chunk only
Embedding✓
BM25 index✓
Reranker✓
Answer generation✓
Citations shown to the user✓

Why so strict? Because the blurb is generated text — usually right, but a model's paraphrase, not the source. Let it leak into answers or citations and you're quoting the model to your users while claiming to quote the document. Index-only gives you the findability without ever polluting provenance: every citation still resolves to real bytes of the real document.

The one non-obvious row is the reranker — it sees the blurb too, and that was learned by measurement, the hard way (§10).

Three honest readings of that calculator. And at query time? Nothing changes.

This surprises people, so plainly: a contextual query costs the same as a plain one and takes the same time. Same single embedding call, same local keyword scan, same fusion. The blurbs live in the index; the answer model reads the original chunks either way, so the answer prompt is byte-for-byte the same size. The entire premium is one-time, at ingestion.

What that one-time premium buys, with measured numbers, is §12 — and whether it's worth it for you is §13.

08Caching, in depth — the whole trick

Every LLM call re-processes your entire prompt from the first token before generating anything. Picture a colleague who, for every question about a 300-page manual, insists on re-reading the manual from page one. Prompt caching is the provider noticing "the first 15,000 tokens of this request are identical to one I handled 20 seconds ago — I still have that work" and skipping the re-read, passing part of the saving back as a discount on those tokens.

Three rules fall out of "it's a prefix cache":

The match starts at token zero. One changed byte early in the prompt — a timestamp, a request ID — and nothing after it can hit.
There's a minimum (typically 1,024 tokens) before caching engages at all.
Entries live minutes, not hours. Caching makes a burst of related calls cheap; it is not storage.

Now look at why contextual retrieval is the perfect caching customer. For one document with 30 chunks, we make 30 calls that all begin identically: 

<document>
...the entire document, byte-identical in all 30 calls...   ← cached after call 1
</document>

Here is the chunk we want to situate...                     ← only this changes

The document rides first — that ordering is Anthropic's published design, and it's not cosmetic. It makes the expensive part of every call (the document) the cacheable part. The document is billed in full exactly once; the other 29 calls pay ~10% for it. That is the entire difference between the $12 and the $2 in the calculator above.

Making it actually hit

Structure matters more than configuration. Keep the cacheable content first and byte-identical (no timestamps or IDs before it). Prime before you parallelize: the cache entry doesn't exist until the first call writes it, so this library runs the first chunk of each document alone, then fans out a small worker pool. And verify rather than assume — every ingestion report carries cached_tokens:

pythonis caching working?
report = rag.ingest_markdown(text, doc_id="report.md")
print(report["context_tokens"], report["cached_tokens"])
61204 48130 ← healthy: ~79% of prompt tokens served from cache 61204 0 ← paying full price. Doc under 1,024 tokens? Behind a load balancer? Legacy Azure deployment? Parallel burst on a short doc? (each has a fix — see docs/caching.md)

Measured on this implementation: 82% of prompt tokens cached in steady state on multi-page documents. One honest wrinkle: providers need a few seconds for a fresh entry to become readable, so on very short documents a parallel burst can finish before the cache settles. The knob is CONTEXT_CONCURRENCY=1 for short-doc corpora; multi-page documents don't care.

09Hybrid search & rank fusion

With contextual text on both indexes, run both retrievers on every query — dense for meaning, BM25 for exact terms — and merge. But merging hides a trap: a cosine similarity of 0.83 and a BM25 score of 7.1 live on incomparable scales. Adding or averaging them is numerology.

The clean merge ignores scores entirely and uses ranks — Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Each chunk earns 1 / (60 + rank) from every list it appears in: 

# chunk ranked 1st by dense, 3rd by BM25:
1/(60+1) + 1/(60+3) = 0.0323   # liked by both → rises
# chunk ranked 1st by dense only:
1/(60+1)            = 0.0164   # loved by one → beaten

A chunk both retrievers liked outranks a chunk either loved alone — which is what you want, given how differently the two fail.

Why 60? Who picked that?

It's the standard constant from the original RRF paper (Cormack et al., 2009). It softens how much the very top ranks dominate, so agreement deeper in the lists still counts. Nobody tunes it; everybody uses it. If you're ever tempted to tune it, you'll get more from improving the blurbs.

A deflationary note from measurement: hybrid is a refinement, not the rescue. On the benchmark corpus, plain-chunk hybrid still scores ~0% — BM25 matches the company name only in title chunks, which contain the name but not the metric, and fusion faithfully inherits the confusion. Garbage ranked two ways is still garbage. The blurb is what makes hybrid worth having.

10Reranking: similarity is not relevance

Retrieval scores how close a chunk is to the query; it can't tell whether the chunk actually answers it. After fusion, the top candidates for a Borealis-Q2-revenue question typically include Borealis Q1's revenue section and Borealis Q2's margin section — all near, only one right. A reranker reads the query and candidates together and reorders by answer-likelihood. One cheap listwise LLM call over the top ~30 fused candidates is enough.

Measured lesson — rerank the contextual text

The reranker must see blurb + chunk, not the bare chunk. An early version of this library showed it bare chunks, and it made results worse — Pass@5 fell from 94% to 81% — because out-of-context chunks are as indistinguishable to a reranker as they were to the retrievers, so it confidently reshuffled twelve identical-looking revenue chunks. Shown the contextual text, the same reranker became the best stage in the pipeline. The lesson generalizes: every stage that judges a chunk needs the chunk's context to judge it.

Engineering note: LLM rerankers are flaky — they garble indices and forget candidates. Treat their output defensively (drop invalid indices, append forgotten candidates in original order): a flaky reranker may reorder your results, but it must never be able to lose one.

11Grounded answers

The last stage hands the top chunks — original text only, per §6 — to the answer model as numbered sources, under a contract: use only these sources; cite inline as [S1], [S2]; if they don't contain the answer, say so plainly. Every [S#] resolves to a document, page, and section, so every claim is checkable.

The contract matters most when retrieval fails. Same question to both pipelines, from a real benchmark run:

Plain pipeline — retrieval failed

"The provided sources do not include Borealis Industries' revenue figures for Q2 2025, so I can't determine how much revenue grew from them alone. [S1] [S3]"

Contextual pipeline — retrieval succeeded

"Borealis Industries' total revenue grew 14% year over year in Q2 2025, reaching $595 million for the quarter [S2]."

Look at the failure carefully: the plain pipeline retrieved the wrong chunks and refused rather than hallucinated. That's the grounding contract holding under failure. Retrieval quality and answer honesty are separate systems — you want both, and this course has only been about the first.

13Should you even use this?

A course that ends in a sales pitch has taught you nothing, so here is the decision, both directions.

Reach for contextual retrieval when…

Your documents are long and state their identity once — filings, contracts, reports, manuals, papers — and your users ask questions that name entities the chunks omit. That's a large fraction of what enterprises actually store, and on it this one-sentence-per-chunk technique is among the highest-leverage changes you can make to a RAG system: a few dollars of one-time ingestion for the numbers in §12, at zero per-query cost.

Stay with plain RAG (or none) when…

Your chunks are already self-contained — FAQ entries, product cards, short notes. The blurb would restate the chunk; save your money (the ten-chunk test in §4 settles this in a minute). Your corpus fits in one prompt — under ~100k tokens, skip retrieval entirely. Your corpus churns hourly — the "one-time" premium isn't one-time anymore. You can't get caching and your documents are very long — set the calculator in §7 to "no caching" and look at the number before deciding.

And if you're unsure: start plain, measure, upgrade on evidence. The library makes that cheap — the benchmark script builds both pipelines side by side, and the offline demo shows the difference without spending a cent. Deciding not to use the technique, on evidence, is a perfectly good outcome of this course.
